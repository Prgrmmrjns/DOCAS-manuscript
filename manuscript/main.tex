\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning glucose models are increasingly embedded in type~1
diabetes (T1D) decision support and closed-loop systems, yet prediction
accuracy alone does not guarantee safe therapeutic use. Observational CGM data
induce strong \emph{covariate bias}: boluses are administered when glucose is
already elevated and are collinear with prandial carbohydrate intake, so the
observational joint distribution conflates the glucose-lowering effect of
insulin with the hyperglycaemic contexts that trigger dosing. Empirical risk
minimization on such data can achieve low error by fitting associative
patterns---future glucose remains high \emph{because} insulin coincides with
high CGM and meals---rather than encoding how glucose would change under
counterfactual bolus perturbations. Diagnosing this with feature attributions
reveals the symptom---e.g., positive SHAP contributions for insulin---but does
not repair the underlying interventional behaviour. We introduce \emph{DOCAS}
(dose--response curve alignment via synthetic augmentation), a lightweight,
model-agnostic post-training procedure that does \emph{not} align feature
attributions. Instead, it aligns the model's \emph{interventional audit
curve}---the response traced by sweeping bolus insulin under a $do(\cdot)$-style
query with context held fixed---to a user-specified \emph{target function}
$\tau$ on hyperglycaemic contexts. DOCAS augments observational data with
synthetic samples labeled by $\tau$ where an intervention is meaningful. In
this study $\tau$ is derived by replaying the insulin pharmacokinetic and
glucose--insulin equations of ReplayBG; the same mechanism accepts arbitrary target functions and
surfaces. On held-out OhioT1DM data from all 12 participants, DOCAS sharply
reduces alignment error relative to a strong LightGBM baseline, a hard
monotone-constrained LightGBM comparator, and a Prendin-style LSTM family
(np-LSTM, p-LSTM), while essentially preserving held-out RMSE. In ReplayBG
digital-twin simulation of corrective-bolus decision support on patient
588---the case study of Prendin et al.---DOCAS LightGBM increases
time-in-range at both horizons and reduces the PH30 hypoglycaemia induced by a
misaligned baseline controller. An ablation study shows the augmentation loop
generalizes to flat, interaction, full-range, and two-dimensional targets.
DOCAS is an open-source Python package in which practitioners supply their
model, intervention feature, context rule, and target function---a more
flexible repair strategy than architecture-specific or monotonicity-only
alternatives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Machine-learning glucose models are increasingly used in type~1 diabetes
(T1D) decision support and public prediction
benchmarks~\citep{oviedo2017,zhu2021,marling2020,beauchamp2021,katsarou2017,rodbard2016}.
Model selection nevertheless remains dominated by held-out prediction error
(RMSE, MAE), even though two models with indistinguishable accuracy can
encode radically different---and clinically incompatible---input--output
relationships~\citep{prendin2023,miller2019,yu2018,hagras2018}. In
observational CGM data, insulin dosing is strongly correlated with concurrent
or antecedent hyperglycaemia and meal intake---a form of \emph{treatment
confounding} familiar from causal
inference~\citep{scholkopf2021}. Doses are delivered in response to glycaemic
state, not assigned experimentally. Under such \emph{covariate bias}, empirical
risk minimization learns associative patterns that predict well on held-out
rows yet fail under interventional use, when a decision support system (DSS)
sweeps candidate bolus doses holding other inputs
fixed~\citep{prendin2023,cappon2023,kushner2020,geirhos2020}. Prendin et al.\
showed that an LSTM with accuracy comparable to a physiologically
preprocessed counterpart assigned \emph{positive} SHAP contributions to
insulin and forecasted glucose \emph{rising} after bolus administration; only
the correctly attributed model improved glycaemic outcomes in corrective-bolus
DSS~\citep{prendin2023,lundberg2017,ribeiro2016,zhu2023xai}. The model can
therefore appear competent on observational metrics while encoding the wrong
interventional dose--response map.

Architectural priors---meal and insulin absorption filters before recurrent
layers~\citep{prendin2023,zecchin2012,mirshekarian2017,pavan2020}---can
partially decouple confounded inputs, but require task-specific design and do
not guarantee correct audit-curve shape on hyperglycaemic contexts.
Kushner et al.\ showed that neural predictors trained for accuracy alone
routinely violate monotonicity constraints and proposed conformance
verification to detect such failures~\citep{kushner2020,kushner2020dt}.

We address this gap with \emph{DOCAS} (dose--response curve alignment via
synthetic augmentation), an open-source Python package for observational models
whose interventional behaviour must match domain priors. DOCAS aligns the
model's \emph{interventional audit curve}---the trajectory $u\mapsto\hat g(x,u)$
obtained by sweeping the intervened feature under a $do(\cdot)$-style query with
context~$x$ held fixed---to a user-specified \emph{target function}
$\tau(x,a)$, not to a \emph{feature attribution} (a scalar such as a SHAP
value for one prediction). The audit curve is exactly the counterfactual a
bolus-sizing DSS reads off when choosing a dose~\citep{pearl2009causality,prendin2023,cappon2023};
$\tau$ is the domain prior for how the forecast \emph{should} change under
that probe. When we write \emph{dose--response curve} without a qualifier, we
mean this generic dose-to-outcome relation and then specify \emph{audit} (model)
or \emph{target} (prior). Rather than modifying model architecture or applying
post-hoc explanation alone, DOCAS \emph{trains} a user-chosen model on
observational rows plus synthetic counterfactuals so that the audit curve
matches $\tau$: the practitioner specifies (i)~which feature is intervened,
(ii)~a context predicate that selects anchor states, and (iii)~$\tau$ at each
anchor and dose level. In T1D we instantiate $\tau$ with a ReplayBG-derived
correction-bolus response on hyperglycaemic anchors
(Section~\ref{subsec:target}), but the same
loop accepts other curves, surfaces, or null constraints
(Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}). The procedure is model-agnostic---here
demonstrated with LightGBM and LSTM~\citep{ke2017}.

Although validated on T1D, DOCAS addresses a general failure mode whenever
treatment variables are confounded with outcome
severity~\citep{scholkopf2021,geirhos2020}. Because target shape, context
selection, and model family are all user-defined, the method is more flexible
than architectural preprocessors, hard monotonicity constraints, or
simulator-coupled training objectives that lock alignment to one network or
one causal graph. Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates the T1D default on
(i)~cohort-wide OhioT1DM forecasting with dose--response alignment metrics,
(ii)~ReplayBG in-silico replay on patient~588, and (iii)~an ablation study
(Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}) that stress-tests alternate targets. The
open-source package exposes the same interface for other domains
(Section~\ref{subsec:software}). Section~\ref{sec:related} contrasts DOCAS with
attribution-based alignment (including causal, do-operator attributions) and
with interventional dose--response estimation
(Table~\ref{tab:alignment-strategies}).
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications for explainable AI and
transfer beyond glucose forecasting.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Prendin et al.\ expose the central failure mode: two LSTM glucose models
with similar RMSE produce opposite corrective-bolus decisions because the
standard model assigns positive SHAP contributions to insulin, whereas a
physiologically structured p-LSTM recovers the expected insulin-lowering and
carbohydrate-raising signs~\citep{prendin2023,lundberg2017}. Related glucose-XAI
studies use attributions diagnostically~\citep{zhu2023xai,cappon2020kdh}. These
works align, or check, a \emph{feature attribution}---a scalar per-feature
importance summarising one prediction. DOCAS instead aligns the model's
\emph{interventional audit curve}, the full trajectory
$u\mapsto\hat g(x,u)$ obtained by intervening on the bolus feature while holding
the hyperglycaemic context fixed. An attribution can carry the correct insulin
sign yet still hide a curve that is flat, non-monotone, or wrongly shaped over
the dose grid a DSS actually sweeps; the audit curve makes that shape the
object of alignment. Moreover, SHAP under dependent features is not a causal
effect, so a correct sign is neither necessary nor sufficient for a correct
counterfactual response; physiological knowledge or interventions are still
needed to define the desired curve~\citep{prendin2023,pearl2019tools,geirhos2020,kushner2020}.

The closest general literature is \emph{explanation-guided learning}, which
feeds feature attributions back into optimization. Right for the Right Reasons
penalizes input gradients on features experts mark as irrelevant
\citep{ross2017}; HINT uses a ranking loss to match gradient-based importance to
human attention~\citep{selvaraju2019}; and CDEP directly penalizes contextual
decomposition attributions that conflict with prior knowledge
\citep{rieger2020}. Erion et al.\ generalize this idea with Expected Gradients
and attribution priors over properties such as sparsity, smoothness, and
biological structure~\citep{erion2021}. These studies establish that
attributions can be differentiable training signals, but they mostly align
feature masks, rankings, or global priors in neural classifiers; they do not
specify a numeric treatment dose--response curve or demonstrate T1D decision
support.

A distinct line of work makes attributions themselves causal by evaluating
them under Pearl's $do$-operator rather than observational
conditioning~\citep{pearl2009causality}. Janzing et al.\ argue that dropping a
feature in a Shapley computation should mean intervening on it,
$\mathbb{E}[Y\mid do(X_S=x_S)]$, not conditioning on
it~\citep{janzing2020causal}; do-Shapley and causal Shapley values build on
this to attribute a model's output to interventions on individual
features~\citep{jung2022doshapley,heskes2020causalshap}. This ``do-SHAP''
family shares our premise---that observational attributions conflate
association with intervention---but differs from DOCAS in three ways. First,
its output is still an \emph{attribution}: a scalar causal contribution per
feature for one prediction, not the full response curve $u\mapsto\hat g(x,u)$ a
DSS sweeps. Second, it is a read-out that \emph{measures} an already-trained
model rather than a training procedure that \emph{repairs} it. Third, it needs
a specified causal graph or SCM and identifiability of the interventional
estimand from data. DOCAS requires neither a causal graph nor identifiability:
because the insulin dose--response effect is not identified from observational
T1D data, we do not estimate $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid do(u)]$ but instead \emph{impose}
a physiological target on the audit curve and fit the model to it. In short, do-SHAP audits an
interventional attribution; DOCAS aligns the interventional curve.

A parallel literature evaluates attributions against known references.
XAI-BENCH uses synthetic data to compute ground-truth Shapley values
\citep{liu2021xaibench}, while OpenXAI scores feature, sign, and rank agreement
with ground-truth explanations alongside faithfulness and stability
\citep{agarwal2022openxai}. This motivates explicit alignment scores, but the
reference differs from ours: those benchmarks test whether an explainer
recovers a known model or data-generating function, whereas DOCAS scores a
model's audit curve against a stated target function~$\tau$.
Because counterfactual responses are not identified from observational T1D data
alone, $\tau$ is a domain prior rather than observed ground truth.

A further body of work aligns models to interventions by \emph{estimating} a
dose--response curve from observational data. Continuous-treatment causal
methods de-confound treatment from covariates---balancing representations,
multi-head dose architectures such as DRNet, or amortised interventional
predictors---to recover $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid do(t)]$ under strong ignorability and
positivity~\citep{schwab2020drnet,scholkopf2021}. DOCAS and these methods both
concern interventional dose--response curves, but from opposite ends: they
\emph{recover} an identifiable estimand from data, whereas DOCAS assumes the
effect is \emph{not} identifiable (insulin is dosed precisely in response to
hyperglycaemia, violating ignorability) and therefore substitutes a stated
target function~$\tau$ for the missing experiment. DOCAS also aligns the working
forecaster the DSS already uses via data augmentation, rather than fitting a
separate causal-effect estimator with its own architecture.

Within glucose forecasting, alignment has instead been imposed through
physiological preprocessing~\citep{prendin2023,zecchin2012,mirshekarian2017},
formal conformance penalties~\citep{kushner2020,kushner2020dt}, and hard
monotonic models~\citep{you2019,runje2023}. LightGBM provides the latter through
monotone constraints~\citep{ke2017}, which we include as a comparator
in Table~\ref{tab:ohio}. Other approaches encode mechanistic inputs
\citep{wolber2025multimodal}, align internal representations to a simulator by
Interchange Intervention Training~\citep{esponera2025}, or constrain a synthetic
CGM generator~\citep{prendin2025mongan}. Outside T1D, expert-gated synthetic
dose--outcome supervision has also been proposed for personalized drug
dosing~\citep{verma2025genex}. Table~\ref{tab:alignment-strategies} summarizes
the main distinctions.

DOCAS is therefore an explanation-guided repair method in spirit, but it aligns
the interventional audit curve to a target function~$\tau$ rather than any
attribution: it does not optimize SHAP values, causal or otherwise. Synthetic
rows train any model against a full user-defined target over the intervention
grid, not only an attribution sign, feature mask, or monotonic split
constraint, and without assuming the effect is identifiable from data. The ReplayBG experiments
(Section~\ref{subsec:replay-results}) then test whether this aligned
counterfactual behaviour improves DSS safety.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

model is trained on observational data (Section~\ref{subsec:baseline}),

interface for T1D glucose forecasting; Section~\ref{subsec:replay-results}

\subsection{Models and OhioT1DM protocol}\label{subsec:baseline}
For each participant we train forecasters $f_b$ that map a feature vector
$x_t$ (recent CGM, insulin, carbohydrate, and time-of-day channels, each
min--max scaled to $[0,1]$) to the change in CGM over a prediction horizon
$h$. The predicted future glucose is
$\hat g_b(x)=\mathrm{CGM}_t+f_b(x)$, evaluated at $h=30$ and $h=60$~min.
The primary backbone is a gradient-boosted regression tree
(LightGBM~\citep{ke2017,friedman2001}). As comparators we also train
(i)~LightGBM with a hard monotone-decreasing constraint on the insulin
feature~\citep{ke2017}, and (ii)~a Prendin-style LSTM family matching
interpretable-DL4BGP~\citep{prendin2023}: np-LSTM (raw bolus/meal impulses;
LSTM(64)+Dense, 100 epochs, batch size 256), p-LSTM (offline IOB/COB
smoothing plus Dense with tanh activation), and DOCAS LSTM (the same
synthetic-augmentation loop applied to the p-LSTM backbone).

Following the OhioT1DM preprocessing of Marling et
al.~\citep{marling2020} and Prendin et al.~\citep{prendin2023}, each
participant's first six monitoring weeks form the training set and the final ten
days the held-out test set; CGM gaps under 30~min in training are linearly
interpolated and test-set gaps are left missing.\label{subsec:ohio-protocol}
Inputs are min--max scaled to
$[0,1]$ using training-set statistics. For LightGBM (and the offline features
fed to p-LSTM / DOCAS LSTM), insulin and carbohydrate channels are converted to
insulin-on-board and carbohydrate-on-board traces (rapid bolus IOB plus active
basal, and meal absorption); np-LSTM instead receives the raw bolus and meal
impulses. The regression target is the
\emph{change} in CGM over the horizon ($\Delta\mathrm{CGM}$), not absolute
future glucose; held-out RMSE is therefore computed on $\Delta\mathrm{CGM}$ in
mg/dL after inverse scaling. We report cohort means $\pm$ standard deviations
across all 12 OhioT1DM participants. These choices differ from Prendin et
al., who evaluated a single compliant subject (patient~588), predicted absolute
future CGM, and scaled features to $[-1,1]$. Pointwise RMSE values are
thus \emph{not} directly comparable across the two studies; we cite Prendin et
al.\ for the attribution--DSS mismatch and replay on patient~588
(Section~\ref{subsec:replay-results}), not for numerical parity of forecasting
error.

\subsection{DOCAS dose--response alignment}\label{subsec:probe}
DOCAS operates on two curves at each anchor context~$x$, selected by a
\emph{context rule} (here baseline-predicted future glucose above
$170$~mg/dL). A \emph{counterfactual probe} fixes $x$, sweeps the intervention
feature $a$ (here bolus insulin $u$) on a dose grid, and reads off model
predictions---Pearl's $do(a)$ query with non-intervened features held at their
values in~$x$~\citep{pearl2009causality}. The \emph{interventional audit
curve} $u\mapsto\hat g_b(x,u)$ is that trajectory: the model's dose--response
map at~$x$, not a scalar feature attribution. The \emph{target function}
$\tau(x,u)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:target}) is the domain prior for how the forecast
\emph{should} change; synthetic rows use $\tau$ as their label.
\emph{Alignment error} is the RMSE (mg/dL) between audit curve and target function
on absolute future-CGM amplitude on held-out hyperglycaemic test contexts
(Section~\ref{subsec:ohio-results}).

We probe each trained model with an additional correction dose: hold the
anchor's observed insulin-on-board (IOB) and all remaining features fixed,
and expose a separate added-dose feature $U_{\max}u$ for $u\in[0,1]$, with
$U_{\max}=10$~U (observational rows keep this channel at zero). The resulting
audit curve is what a bolus-sizing DSS reads off. In principle it
should slope downward; in practice it often does not, because insulin coincides
with hyperglycaemia in observational data and the learned audit curve is
frequently flat, noisy, or even
rising~\citep{bergman1979,cobelli2009,prendin2023}. Because this effect is not
identified from confounded observational data, we do not estimate it; we
specify $\tau(x,u)$ and train the model so its audit curve matches it.

The fix is to teach the model a mechanistic target curve. On hyperglycaemic
contexts---those whose baseline-predicted future glucose exceeds $170$~mg/dL,
just above the $140$--$180$~mg/dL time-in-range
ceiling~\citep{battelino2019}---we derive $\tau(x,u)$ from ReplayBG's
single-meal T1D model~\citep{cappon2023}.\label{subsec:target}
In implementation units, its insulin and glucose states obey
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot I_{\mathrm{sc1}}&=-k_d I_{\mathrm{sc1}}+I(t-\gamma),&
\dot I_{\mathrm{sc2}}&=k_d I_{\mathrm{sc1}}-k_{a2}I_{\mathrm{sc2}},\\
\dot I_p&=k_{a2}I_{\mathrm{sc2}}-k_eI_p,&
\dot X&=-p_2X+p_2\frac{SI}{V_I}(I_p-I_{pb}),\\
\dot G&=-[S_G+\rho(G)X]G+S_GG_b+Ra/V_G,&
\dot{IG}&=(G-IG)/\alpha ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:replay-insulin}
\end{equation}
where the observable $IG$ is interstitial glucose and $\gamma=8$~min is the
subcutaneous-input delay. We reproduce ReplayBG's one-minute backward-Euler
updates exactly. A dose $U_{\max}u$ is delivered as the same one-minute
$1000U_{\max}u/w$ mU/kg correction pulse used by the replay controller for
body weight $w$; all states start at basal steady state with
$G=IG=G_{\mathrm{ref}}=220$~mg/dL.

Let $IG_h(u;w)$ be the resulting interstitial glucose at horizon $h$. We
subtract the matched no-bolus trajectory so that the simulator supplies only
the incremental insulin effect, while the fitted baseline retains each
anchor's meals and glucose trend:
\begin{equation}
  R_h(u;w)=\frac{IG_h(0;w)-IG_h(u;w)}{G_{\mathrm{ref}}},
  \qquad
  \tau(x,u)=\hat g_b(x)\,[1-R_h(u;w)] .
  \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
The simulator parameters are fixed before OhioT1DM fitting at the modes of the
ReplayBG priors used in our replay pipeline ($S_G=0.0174$~min$^{-1}$,
$p_2=0.11$~min$^{-1}$, $SI=1.15\times10^{-3}$,
$k_{a2}=0.0114$~min$^{-1}$, and $k_d=0.0204$~min$^{-1}$); the remaining
parameters use ReplayBG defaults. Only measured body weight varies by
participant. At $w=75$~kg and $U_{\max}=10$~U, this recurrence gives
$R_{30}(1)=0.040$ and $R_{60}(1)=0.347$. Thus the PH30 target falls gradually,
whereas the PH60 response is much larger and becomes steeper at high dose;
this behaviour is generated by the simulator rather than imposed with a
hyperbolic shape (Figure~\ref{fig:target}).

  \textbf{Left:} absolute target future CGM

To turn this target into training signal, we form candidate
$(x,u)$ pairs on hyperglycaemic anchors with an evenly spaced interventional
grid $u\in[0,1]$, label each with~\eqref{eq:target}, and append the resulting
synthetic rows to the observational training set.\label{subsec:augment}
A user-chosen model (here LightGBM or p-LSTM) is then fit on the combined real
and synthetic data with a fixed synthetic-row weight, so it learns the
target-curve shape on hyperglycaemic contexts while retaining accuracy on the
original rows.
Optional residual-correction passes reweight the synthetic labels toward the
current model's residual on the audit grid. Because $R_h$ is taken from ReplayBG
prior-mode parameters (with only body weight varying by participant), the
mechanistic amplitude is not tuned against the forecasting loss: the model
cannot ``win'' by flattening $\tau$. Alignment in Table~\ref{tab:ohio} is
reported as absolute future-CGM RMSE in mg/dL.

\subsection{Software interface}\label{subsec:software}
DOCAS is an open-source Python package that exposes the pipeline in
Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart} through a small, composable API. The user supplies
four objects: (i)~a supervised model with standard \texttt{fit}/\texttt{predict}
methods such as gradient-boosted trees or neural nets; (ii)~the intervention feature to perturb when generating
counterfactual rows; (iii)~a \emph{context rule} that selects anchor states
where alignment should apply (in T1D, baseline-predicted future glucose above
$170$~mg/dL); and (iv)~a \emph{target function} $\tau(x,a)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:target})
that maps each anchor $x$ and intervention level $a$ to the desired outcome
label for synthetic supervision.

The core operation is the same in every use case: train a baseline on
observational $(x,y)$ pairs, sweep the intervention grid on anchors selected
by the context rule, evaluate $\tau$ on each $(x,a)$ pair, append the
resulting synthetic rows, and train a new model on the combined data. \emph{How} $\tau$ is chosen
is flexible. When domain knowledge already fixes the target completely---a
null curve, a prescribed interaction surface, or simulator-derived response
taken literally---the user can \emph{hardcode} $\tau$ and run a single
augmentation and training pass; Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}
(``hardcoded'' regime) illustrates this mode, and the main OhioT1DM
experiments use the same pattern with Eq.~\ref{eq:target} held fixed.
Nothing in this interface is
T1D-specific: the ReplayBG-derived insulin curve is one bundled preset, and
Section~\ref{subsec:ablation} demonstrates the same entry point with flat,
interaction, full-range, and multi-feature targets. This paper reports the T1D preset and
evaluation protocol; the package itself is domain-agnostic.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{OhioT1DM forecasting}\label{subsec:ohio-results}

We evaluated six forecasters on the OhioT1DM dataset~\citep{marling2020}
under the protocol in Section~\ref{subsec:ohio-protocol}: baseline LightGBM,
LightGBM with a hard monotone-decreasing constraint on the insulin
feature~\citep{ke2017}, DOCAS LightGBM, and a Prendin-style LSTM family
(np-LSTM, p-LSTM, and DOCAS LSTM) matching the Keras LSTM(64), 100-epoch,
batch-256 setup of interpretable-DL4BGP~\citep{prendin2023}.
Table~\ref{tab:ohio} reports cohort-mean held-out test RMSE and
\emph{alignment error}: the RMSE between each model's interventional audit
curve and the ReplayBG-derived target curve
(Sections~\ref{subsec:probe} and~\ref{subsec:target}) on hyperglycaemic test
contexts.
Baseline LightGBM RMSE ($\approx22.5$ and $35.6$~mg/dL at PH30 and PH60) is in the
same ballpark as the single-subject Ohio results reported by Prendin et
al.~\citep{prendin2023}, but the two forecasting evaluations should not be read
as a head-to-head replication because they differ in cohort scope (12 vs.\ one
participant), prediction target ($\Delta\mathrm{CGM}$ vs.\ absolute future CGM),
and input representation.
Despite this observational accuracy, the unaligned LightGBM model retained
substantial alignment error ($14.3\pm5.5$ and $29.9\pm15.3$~mg/dL).
Figure~\ref{fig:drc} shows why: cohort-mean baseline audit curves are
nearly flat or non-monotone across the insulin grid, consistent with the
confounded insulin signatures reported by Prendin et al.\ and Kushner et
al.~\citep{prendin2023,kushner2020}. Hard monotone constraints cut LightGBM
alignment error only partially ($10.7\pm6.6$ and $21.8\pm6.9$~mg/dL) while
slightly raising RMSE ($22.9\pm2.5$ and $36.0\pm4.0$~mg/dL). DOCAS reduced
LightGBM alignment error to $3.5\pm1.9$ and $5.7\pm1.8$~mg/dL at PH30 and PH60
while essentially preserving held-out RMSE ($22.4\pm2.8$ and $35.5\pm4.3$~mg/dL).
Individual participant audit curves (faint lines in
Figure~\ref{fig:drc}) confirm that alignment gains are cohort-wide rather than
driven by a single subject.
Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart} shows the fixed
ReplayBG-derived target~$\tau$ (PH30/PH60 kinetic responses) and the synthetic
rows DOCAS appends to observational training data for a representative
participant.

\subsection{ReplayBG simulation}\label{subsec:replay-results}

Forecasting metrics alone do not establish clinical utility when models drive
therapy. We therefore embedded baseline and DOCAS LightGBM controllers in the
ReplayBG digital-twin framework~\citep{cappon2023}, replaying postprandial
windows from patient~588---the compliant subject used by Prendin et
al.~\citep{prendin2023}. For each eligible eight-hour window, ReplayBG
identifies a personalized physiological model from observed insulin,
carbohydrate, and CGM traces and simulates counterfactual glucose trajectories
under alternative bolus policies; windows with twin RMSE above
$25$~mg/dL are excluded from Table~\ref{tab:replay}. The corrective-bolus DSS
follows Prendin et al.: triggered two hours after meal announcement when
simulated CGM exceeds $180$~mg/dL, it selects the dose on a $0$--$10$~U grid that
minimizes $(\hat g-120)^2+10u^2$ using the controller's PH-step forecast,
scales the delivered bolus by a horizon gain
$\sqrt{R_{30}(1)/R_h(1)}$ derived from the same ReplayBG target~$\tau$
(gain~$1$ at PH30; $\approx0.34$ at PH60), and administers no bolus when
additional insulin does not lower the predicted future glucose relative to no
correction (the behaviour seen with Prendin et al.'s misaligned np-LSTM).

Without decision support (No DS), mean TIR was $49.5\%$ with no
time-below-range (TBR; Table~\ref{tab:replay})---a low mean driven by one
severely hyperglycaemic eligible window. The \emph{baseline} controller
---despite similar pointwise forecasting skill---raised mean TIR to
$73.2\%$ at PH30 with $4.5\pm0.7$~U of corrective insulin, but introduced
hypoglycaemia (mean TBR $3.1\pm6.3\%$, up to $12.5\%$ in an individual window).
Where its audit curve shows only local, non-monotone predicted dips, the
Prendin grid search still issues boluses; in other misaligned contexts it
would withhold CIB as in Prendin et al.'s np-LSTM~\citep{prendin2023}. At PH60
the horizon gain damps delivered doses ($1.9\pm0.5$~U); baseline TBR returned
to $0\%$ while mean TIR remained $71.9\%$.

The \emph{DOCAS} controller achieved the intended alignment benefit. At PH30
it raised mean TIR to $74.2\%$ versus $73.2\%$ for baseline, delivered slightly
less corrective insulin ($4.3\pm3.5$~U vs.\ $4.5\pm0.7$~U), and reduced mean TBR
from $3.1\pm6.3\%$ to $2.6\pm5.2\%$. At PH60 its monotone audit curve selected
larger raw grid optima that survive the shared horizon gain
($4.0\pm2.0$~U vs.\ $1.9\pm0.5$~U), raising mean TIR to $75.3\%$ versus $71.9\%$
for baseline while keeping TBR at $0\%$ and cutting TAR. Figure~\ref{fig:patient588-effect}
dissects a representative postprandial
window for patient~588 at PH30 and PH60. The upper panels show simulated CGM
after the corrective-bolus decision-support (CIB) trigger (vertical dotted line,
two hours post-meal): the baseline controller issues $4.0$~U at PH30 and
undershoots into hypoglycaemia, whereas DOCAS delivers $2.5$~U and remains
inside the $70$--$180$~mg/dL band; at PH60 both arms stay in range
($1.5$~U baseline vs.\ $3.5$~U DOCAS). The lower panels explain \emph{why}: at
the same decision context (simulated CGM $\approx184$~mg/dL, identical IOB and
meal state), the baseline audit curve is non-monotone and predicts
\emph{rising} future glucose at higher corrective doses---yet local dips still
let the CIB optimizer select a large bolus to chase the $120$~mg/dL target.
The DOCAS model instead predicts monotone glucose lowering across the insulin
grid at both PH30 and PH60, yielding the safer PH30 bolus and the stronger
PH60 correction seen in the upper panels. These results support the Prendin
et al.\ conclusion that a correctly shaped audit curve, not RMSE alone,
determines DSS safety---while showing that alignment can be achieved
\emph{post hoc} via synthetic target-curve augmentation rather than only
through architectural preprocessing.

\subsection{Ablation: flexibility of synthetic-target alignment}\label{subsec:ablation}

The results above use one physiologically motivated target function: the
ReplayBG-derived insulin~$\tau$. In the DOCAS package
(Section~\ref{subsec:software}), that curve is merely a default \emph{preset}; the augmentation loop itself is agnostic to target
choice---it fits whichever target function or surface the user supplies on
contexts matched by the context rule. We probe this flexibility with five ablations on
patient~588 at PH30 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}), each
starting from the same baseline model and deliberately using simple
non-ReplayBG targets.

\emph{Hardcoded hyperbolic target.} Forcing a fixed hyperbolic target (no
ReplayBG kinetics) drives alignment error to $2.9$~mg/dL---comparable to the
main DOCAS model's $3.8$~mg/dL on the same participant---but at an RMSE cost of
$+5.4$~mg/dL, whereas ReplayBG-preset DOCAS essentially preserves RMSE on the
cohort (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}, panel~1; Table~\ref{tab:ohio}).
A physiologically scaled $\tau$ therefore buys most of the alignment benefit
without the accuracy cost of an aggressive synthetic amplitude; brute-force
fitting can always buy more alignment, but the price is a model that no longer
tracks real glucose dynamics away from the synthetic grid.

\emph{Feature independence.} Setting the target amplitude to zero forces a
perfectly flat target function: insulin is given \emph{no} effect on the forecast
whatsoever (panel~2). The fitted audit curve reproduces this null shape almost
exactly (alignment error $1.5$~mg/dL) at a small RMSE cost ($+1.0$~mg/dL),
confirming that DOCAS can impose feature independence, not only a specific
monotone shape, when that is the desired constraint.

\emph{Insulin--CGM interaction (indirect effect).} Rather than shifting the
forecast directly, we let insulin modulate \emph{how strongly} the current
CGM value drives the forecast: the target sets
$\partial\hat g/\partial\,\mathrm{CGM}=1$ at zero insulin (full pass-through)
and $1-\delta_{30}=0.85$ at the maximum corrective dose. The fitted model
recovers this slope schedule almost exactly ($1.00\!\to\!0.86$ learned vs.\
$1.00\!\to\!0.85$ target; panel~3), showing that the same mechanism can encode
indirect, interaction-type physiological priors---insulin's effect need not
act on the forecast in isolation, but can act on another feature's effect.

\emph{Two-dimensional insulin--CHO surface.} Finally we combine a hyperbolic
insulin-lowering effect with a linear carbohydrate-driven rise in a single
target surface, $\tau(x,u,c)=f_0(x)\bigl(1+\delta(c_{\mathrm{norm}}-r(u))\bigr)$
(panel~4). The fitted surface tracks the target closely (surface
RMSE $4.8$~mg/dL) at a modest RMSE cost ($+1.2$~mg/dL), demonstrating that
target-curve alignment extends beyond one-dimensional insulin curves to joint
multi-feature target surfaces.

\emph{Full glycaemic range.} Restricting synthetic anchors to hyperglycaemia
($f_0>170$~mg/dL) is intentional: the same hyperbolic $\tau$ applied across the
full band down to the time-in-range floor ($f_0>70$~mg/dL) reaches strong
full-band alignment ($2.5$~mg/dL) but at an RMSE cost of $+5.8$~mg/dL---on par
with aggressive hyper-only hardcoded fitting and far above the near-zero
ReplayBG-preset DOCAS RMSE delta (panel~5). Extending alignment into
normoglycaemia therefore buys
little clinically for corrective-bolus DSS (which triggers only above
$180$~mg/dL) while further eroding observational forecasting skill; the
hyperglycaemia-only context rule is what keeps the RMSE--alignment trade-off
manageable.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This study demonstrates that observational glucose models can be
accurate yet \emph{interventionally wrong}: they fit CGM trajectories while
learning associative patterns induced by covariate bias between insulin, meals,
and glycaemic state---high insulin predicts high future glucose because boluses
are delivered into hyperglycaemic, meal-overlapping
contexts~\citep{prendin2023,kushner2020}. SHAP and related
explainability tools---including causal, do-operator attributions such as
do-SHAP~\citep{janzing2020causal,jung2022doshapley}---are necessary to
\emph{diagnose} such failures but, by themselves, only \emph{measure} the model
and do not repair the learned interventional
mapping~\citep{lundberg2017,miller2019}. DOCAS complements explanation with
\emph{alignment}: it matches the model's interventional audit curve to a stated
target function~$\tau$. Synthetic rows sampled along $\tau$ teach the audit
curve how future glucose should change when insulin is varied holding other
features fixed---the counterfactual probe $do(\mathrm{insulin}=u)$ that a
bolus-sizing DSS actually issues~\citep{pearl2009causality,prendin2023,cappon2023}.

The target in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:replay-insulin}--\eqref{eq:target}
encodes the same delayed subcutaneous absorption, remote insulin action,
glucose kinetics, and interstitial lag used in the downstream ReplayBG
evaluation~\citep{cappon2023}. This matters qualitatively: ReplayBG predicts
little correction-bolus effect by 30 minutes but a much larger effect by
60 minutes, rather than a dose curve that falls most steeply immediately and
then saturates. By restricting supervision to
hyperglycaemic contexts above $170$~mg/dL, DOCAS focuses alignment where
corrective semantics apply and avoids imposing large artificial drops on
normoglycaemic states. The resulting curves preserve the monotonicity
properties Kushner et al.\ verify formally in neural
predictors~\citep{kushner2020} while retaining simulator-derived
horizon and dose dependence.

Table~\ref{tab:ohio} makes these contrasts quantitative on the same Ohio
protocol. Hard monotone LightGBM constraints reduce PH30 alignment error only
from $14.3$ to $10.7$~mg/dL; p-LSTM's physiological IOB/COB filters reach
$7.7$~mg/dL; DOCAS LightGBM and DOCAS LSTM reach $3.5$ and $1.9$~mg/dL
respectively, with a similar ordering at PH60. Relative to architectural
approaches such as the physiological preprocessing layer in
p-LSTM~\citep{prendin2023,zecchin2012,mirshekarian2017}, DOCAS is
model-agnostic and optimizes the interventional audit curve directly without
redesigning the network---and still improves the already-filtered p-LSTM
backbone. Relative to monotonicity constraints,
mechanistic LightGBM feature engineering~\citep{wolber2025multimodal}, or
conformance penalties~\citep{kushner2020,ke2017}, it accepts arbitrary target
curves---not only directional signs---and separates \emph{where} to align
(context rule) from \emph{what} to align ($\tau$). Relative to
full digital-twin or PINN hybrids~\citep{cappon2023,dallaman2007}, it requires
no online identification of differential-equation parameters. Packaging DOCAS
as an open-source library makes this flexibility explicit: practitioners in
other domains can plug in their own target and context logic while reusing the
same augmentation loop.

Beyond T1D, the DOCAS software interface formalizes a general recipe for
injecting prior knowledge into black-box models under covariate bias:
(1)~specify the intervention feature; (2)~define a context rule and target
function~$\tau$ from domain theory; (3)~augment observational training data
with synthetic $(x,a,\tau)$ tuples sampled on selected anchors; and
(4)~validate audit-curve alignment on held-out counterfactual probes, not only
on observational error. Because model, context, and target are all
user-supplied, the same code path covers pharmacological dose-finding with
limited interventional data~\citep{scholkopf2021}, critical-care vasopressor
titration, and settings where scanner metadata confounds
predictions~\citep{zech2018,degris2024}---each with a different $\tau$ and
anchor predicate. Process-driven synthetic data are already accepted in drug
development when they preserve decision-relevant
inferences~\citep{cobelli2009}; DOCAS applies the same philosophy at the
level of \emph{model behaviour} rather than dataset generation.

The cost of alignment is explicit in our T1D experiments, but with the
ReplayBG preset it is small: cohort-mean RMSE is essentially unchanged when
alignment is imposed---a compromise in the same spirit as the small accuracy gap between
p-LSTM and np-LSTM in Prendin et al., which nonetheless produced large DSS
differences~\citep{prendin2023}---but that cost is not uniform under more
aggressive targets (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}). Per-participant
RMSE deltas and alignment gains vary widely (Figure~\ref{fig:drc}), and it
remains unclear why alignment is comparatively cheap for some patients yet
demands a larger forecasting penalty for others. Likely drivers include how
strongly insulin is confounded with hyperglycaemia in each subject, how much
observational trajectories already resemble the imposed target, and broader
cohort heterogeneity in dosing behaviour and glycaemic variability; we do not yet
know which of these dominates. For decision support where a single erroneous
bolus can cause hypoglycaemia, a bounded trade-off is clinically acceptable when
the alignment benefit is real~\citep{battelino2019,nimri2020}, but understanding
when that compromise is unavoidable remains an open question.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
In the present T1D preset, alignment is deliberately \emph{partial}: synthetic
anchors and the audit probe are restricted to hyperglycaemic contexts
($f_0>170$~mg/dL), so the interventional curve is not constrained under
normoglycaemia or mild elevation. Extending $\tau$ across the full band down to
the time-in-range floor ($f_0>70$~mg/dL) improves full-band alignment but
inflates held-out RMSE by $\approx+6$~mg/dL on patient~588
(Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}), versus a near-zero RMSE delta for
hyperglycaemia-only ReplayBG-preset DOCAS---confirming that broader context
coverage competes
directly with forecasting skill where a corrective-bolus DSS does not need it.
The current target uses ReplayBG prior-mode parameters and
patient body weight, but not patient-specific twinned insulin sensitivity,
meal state, or the age distribution implicit in the IOB feature. It therefore
represents a newly delivered correction as a separate added-dose channel
alongside observed IOB, matching the replay controller probe but remaining a
reproducible
population prior rather than a fully individualized digital twin.
Future work could condition the target on prospectively identified ReplayBG
parameters and latent state~\citep{cappon2023,schiavon2018}. ReplayBG
evaluation is currently
restricted to patient~588; extending to additional OhioT1DM subjects and to
prospective closed-loop trials remains necessary. DOCAS does not replace causal
identification when unobserved confounders dominate---SHAP attributions can
still mislead on individual instances, as Prendin et al.\ note for exercise
and illness~\citep{prendin2023}. Finally, synthetic augmentation assumes the target function~$\tau$ is correct;
misspecified priors could imprint a wrong interventional response as readily as
observational bias imprints a wrong associational one.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
A first priority is to narrow any residual RMSE--alignment trade-off under
aggressive or misspecified targets.
Our T1D ReplayBG preset already obtains tight audit-curve alignment at
negligible forecasting cost; it remains open whether richer target families,
multi-horizon targets, or staged
fitting that separates alignment from accuracy tuning can keep that property
when $\tau$ is less physiologically matched. Closely
related, we need to explain the large per-patient spread in that compromise:
why alignment is near-costless for some OhioT1DM participants but expensive for
others, and how subject-level confounding strength, baseline misalignment, and
cohort heterogeneity jointly determine where synthetic augmentation can correct
behaviour without eroding forecasting skill. Second, we plan to apply the same
synthetic-augmentation interface beyond glucose forecasting to other biomedical
datasets where observational training induces covariate bias: treatment timing or
dose collinear with severity (intensive care, oncology dosing,
pharmacovigilance), behavioural interventions delivered when risk is already
elevated (readmission, deterioration alerts), and any setting where a model must
answer a counterfactual ``what if we changed treatment?'' rather than only fit
associative patterns. Third, runtime remains a practical barrier for heavier
architectures: each residual-correction pass retrains on real plus synthetic
rows, which is manageable for gradient-boosted trees but slower for neural
nets. Faster variants could cache and warm-start repeated fits or substitute
lightweight proxies during development. Finally, prospective
validation---embedding DOCAS in live or shadow-mode decision support and
extending ReplayBG-style replay beyond patient~588---is needed before clinical
deployment.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented DOCAS, an open-source Python package for synthetic-augmentation
alignment that trains a new model on real and synthetic rows toward a
user-specified target function~$\tau$. In type~1 diabetes we instantiate
$\tau$ as a ReplayBG-derived correction-bolus response; on OhioT1DM, DOCAS
outperforms baseline LightGBM, monotone-constrained LightGBM, and Prendin
np-/p-LSTM on held-out audit-curve alignment while essentially preserving
forecasting accuracy on both tree and LSTM backbones, and in ReplayBG
simulation an aligned LightGBM controller increases time-in-range and reduces
hypoglycaemia relative to an equally accurate but misaligned baseline. Because
practitioners choose the model, context rule, and target function, DOCAS is
more flexible than architecture-locked or monotonicity-only repair strategies.
For decision support and hybrid closed-loop systems, validating interventional
audit curves against stated target functions should be as routine as reporting
RMSE; DOCAS offers a reusable path to that validation wherever domain knowledge
specifies how predictions ought to change under intervention.
